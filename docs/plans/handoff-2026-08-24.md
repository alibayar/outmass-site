## The strategic finding: Microsoft is shipping mail merge

Roadmap item **423047**, "Outlook: Mail Merge (Advanced) on Outlook on the Web
and new Outlook for Windows". Verified against Microsoft's own release feed on
24 August: status **In development**, **preview August 2026**, **GA September
2026**, entry last modified 29 May.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

**The threat.** "Mail merge in Outlook" is becoming a free Microsoft feature.
It cannot stay our headline claim. What survives is everything after Send: who
opened, who clicked, who replied, follow-ups that stop on reply, pacing that
protects the sender, campaign reports. Microsoft has published **nothing** about
whether the Advanced feature will do any of that — silence, not denial, and we
write it that way.

**The opening.** Word's mail merge does not work in new Outlook at all: it needs
MAPI, which only classic Outlook exposes. Merges that ran for years broke
silently when tenants were moved, and the people affected are searching right
now. Every page answering them quotes a roadmap date that has already passed.
That gap is what the first blog post was written into.

Both halves of this belong in the 0.2.3 store-listing review, and the broad
Google Alert (`"mail merge" "new outlook"`) is now our early warning for the day
the feature actually ships.

## Three things that were caught lying

**The pricing page.** Writing the plan lines meant checking every gate in code,
and three did not match: click tracking was sold as Starter but gated nowhere,
campaign reports appeared only under Pro and are ungated, and email templates
were shown as Pro-only when `templates.py` returns `required_plan: "starter"` —
we were **underselling Starter to its own buyers**. Fixed by moving the page to
the product, never the reverse: enforcing those gates would take working
features away from free users (`0ded8fa`).

**The sitemap.** `docs/sitemap.xml` declared a namespace that does not exist
(`sitemap-protocols.com`). Search Console had flagged "Hatalı ad alanı — Satır
2, Etiket: urlset" on every read since 29 April — four months. Fixed, re-submitted,
now **Başarılı, 15 pages**.

**My own review script.** The second review reported two findings and no
survivors. The lens agents had actually produced twenty-four; a flattening error
sent the skeptics the string `"undefined"`, which they correctly refuted. Reading
the journal instead of the summary is the only reason the post did not publish
with its flagship claim inverted — Microsoft's page says a distribution group in
the organisation's address book **does** count as one recipient, while a personal
Contact Group does not. I had written the opposite as the article's thesis.

## Five things this week taught

1. **The artifact outranks the report.** A published sitemap, a store's public
   page, Microsoft's own roadmap feed — each corrected something we believed
   from a dashboard or a summary.
2. **Read the journal, not the summary.** Two of this week's worst near-misses
   were tools reporting success: a workflow that said "no findings" and a green
   report that said "none yet".
3. **Measure before hypothesising.** "Google will not index these pages because
   they are thin" died in one command: 2,278–2,536 words each, and the rejected
   one is the most internally linked page on the site.
4. **The letter of a trigger is not its spirit.** Ali caught that one, and it is
   now written into both the backlog and memory.
5. **Verify claims about competitors as strictly as claims about ourselves.**
   The secondary sources on Microsoft's roadmap were all wrong about the dates,
   and the one claim I published without checking the primary source was the one
   that inverted.
