Outlook Mail Merge Limit: How to Send 1000+ Emails Without Blocks

Published April 29, 2026 · updated October 11, 2026 · 7 min read

You write a campaign, hit Send on 1,200 personalized emails, and somewhere partway through Outlook stops cooperating. New messages disappear into the Outbox. Then a non-delivery report arrives: "The message can't be submitted because the sender's submission quota was exceeded." Welcome to Microsoft's mail-merge limit.

This post covers exactly what the limit is, why it exists, what triggers it, and the realistic ways to send 1,000+ personalized emails per day from an Outlook account without getting throttled or — worse — flagged for abuse.

The actual numbers

Microsoft publishes business-plan limits at learn.microsoft.com/exchange-online-limits and consumer limits at support.microsoft.com/outlook/sending-limits. Stripped to the essentials — with the cells Microsoft does not publish left as such, rather than guessed:

Account type Recipients/day Messages/min Recipients/message
Outlook.com (free) Not published Not published Not published
Outlook.com with a Microsoft 365 subscription 5,000 (1,000 of them to people you have never emailed) Not published 500
Microsoft 365 Business Basic / Standard / Premium 10,000 30 Up to 1,000, set by your admin (500 by default)
Exchange Online (standalone) 10,000 30 Up to 1,000, set by your admin (500 by default)
New Microsoft 365 tenant (first 60 days) Lower tenant-wide limit on external recipients (10%, then 25% of the standard quota) 30 As above
Trial tenants Capped for the whole tenant; Microsoft's own pages give different figures 30 As above

Three numbers in tension:

  • Recipients per day — your hard ceiling. Hitting it on a work or school mailbox returns a "submission quota exceeded" report (5.2.2); a personal Outlook.com account may instead be paused until you verify it.
  • Messages per minute — a throughput throttle. Microsoft holds the extra messages and sends them over the following minutes.
  • Recipients per message — caps the To/CC/BCC fields on a single message.

For a personalized campaign you're sending one message per recipient (one address in To:), so the per-message cap doesn't matter. The two that do are per day and per minute.

Why these limits exist

Microsoft is balancing two pressures: legitimate business email throughput vs. spam/phishing prevention. If a compromised account could send 100,000 emails/hour, criminals would weaponise every breach. The 30/minute cap is anti-abuse infrastructure. The 10,000/day cap is policy.

New tenants start lower. Microsoft's tenant-wide limit on external recipients is set to 10% of the standard calculated quota while a tenant is less than 31 days old, and 25% from day 31 to day 60, per Microsoft's Exchange team post on tenant outbound email limits. It is not a per-mailbox figure like the other rows in the table: it covers the whole tenant and counts only external recipients.

Important. Microsoft's limits are not the same as your inbox-placement limits. You can stay under every number in the table above — and still watch every single cold email go to spam. Anti-spam filters at the receiving end (Gmail, Yahoo, corporate domains) have their own thresholds, and they're stricter than Microsoft's.

What actually happens when you exceed the limit

Per-day limit

On a Microsoft 365 work or school mailbox, Microsoft returns a non-delivery report (NDR) with code 5.2.2, "Submission quota exceeded"; its help page for the error reads "The message can't be submitted because the sender's submission quota was exceeded." Future sends get the same answer until the number of recipients you have messaged in the past 24 hours drops below the limit. It is a rolling window, not a reset at a fixed hour. The account itself is not suspended: nothing sends, and you find the NDR if you go looking.

On a personal Outlook.com account, Microsoft may instead pause sending and ask you to verify the account, with instructions sent to your Outlook inbox. Follow them before you send again. OutMass stops the campaign when this happens and shows the same instruction, and you press Resume sending once the account is verified.

Per-minute limit

Microsoft throttles rather than refuses: in its own words, the excess is "throttled and successively carried over to the following minutes," which "will typically not block the sender's account." A tool that submits over SMTP has to retry the messages that were turned away.

Burst patterns trigger separate anti-abuse systems

Even if you stay under the published limits, sending 200 near-identical messages in a few seconds looks automated. Microsoft says Exchange Online "isn't suited to accommodate bulk-mailing scenarios," and that Outlook.com limits vary with an account's usage history. In our experience, personal Outlook.com accounts, new ones especially, can be stopped well below the published numbers. Avoiding bursts isn't optional.

How to send 1000+ emails without hitting the wall

Stay below the per-minute throughput

Microsoft's documented cap for business mailboxes is 30 messages a minute, which is one message every 2 seconds. Treat that as a ceiling, not a target. For a large list, spreading the campaign over several days with a daily cap keeps you further from the daily limit.

Tools like OutMass pace sends for you. From a Microsoft 365 work or school mailbox, OutMass sends about one message every 2 seconds. From a personal Outlook.com account, campaigns, follow-ups and A/B test winners use a gentler pace (a campaign goes a few messages at a time), because Microsoft publishes no limits for free accounts and holds new ones lower; the bigger a campaign's daily batch or an A/B test winner, the closer the gaps get to 2 seconds.

Spread campaigns across multiple days

If your list is 5,000 recipients and you're on a Microsoft 365 Business plan (10,000/day cap), you can technically send the whole thing in a day. You shouldn't. A 5,000-message daily volume from an account that normally sends 50 messages/day is exactly the pattern that lights up Microsoft's anti-abuse dashboards. Split the list:

  • Day 1: 500 messages (warmup)
  • Day 2: 1,000
  • Day 3: 1,500
  • Day 4: 2,000

That's a normal sales operator's sending pattern. Microsoft doesn't blink, recipient mail servers don't blink, your reputation builds.

Personalize past the first line

Spam filters compare messages. If 1,000 messages have the same body except for {{firstName}} at the top, they are still about 99% the same, and they read as one bulk mailing. Effective personalization changes more than a name:

  • Different first sentences ("I noticed you just launched X" vs "Saw your team's recent post on Y").
  • Reference different facts about the recipient's company.
  • Vary the call-to-action between two or three options (a direct meeting, a reply with a question, a link).

AI email writers help here — up to a point. OutMass's AI Writer (Claude-powered) drafts a polished subject and body from a short prompt, using the standard {{firstName}}-style merge tags. For genuinely per-recipient text — a custom opening line, a referenced detail — add a column to your CSV with that line written per recipient and merge it in; the combination beats either approach alone.

Use a real, working unsubscribe link

Spam complaints count against a sender, and the fastest way to lower them is to make unsubscribing easy: a link in every message. Recipients who would have pressed "Report spam" press Unsubscribe instead. OutMass adds that link to every email and skips anyone who used it in your later campaigns.

Skip attachments for cold outreach

Attachments are the single biggest deliverability killer for cold mail. A 1 MB PDF attached to an unrecognized sender is treated like a phishing attempt by most modern filters. The same PDF as a OneDrive sharing link — the sender's name on the link, no payload in the message — sails through.

If 10,000/day still isn't enough

You've hit the policy ceiling. Three real options, all of them changing the architecture rather than working around limits:

Option 1: Multiple sending accounts (carefully)

Some companies set up several Microsoft 365 mailboxes — sales@, outreach@, individual reps — and split the campaign across them. This works but only if each mailbox is a real, signed-off address with a real human attached. Accounts created only to get around the limit are exactly what anti-abuse systems look for, and they put the whole domain's reputation at risk.

Option 2: A transactional email service

For genuine high-volume marketing or transactional traffic, the right tool is a dedicated transactional email service: Azure Communication Services, SendGrid, Mailgun, Postmark, Amazon SES. They're built for the 50,000+ messages/day case, with their own deliverability infrastructure, IP warming, and bounce/complaint handling.

The trade-off: messages send from noreply@yourdomain.com, not from your personal Outlook mailbox. Recipients can tell it's a marketing platform. For cold outreach where personal-looking matters, this is a downgrade. For transactional or newsletter mail, it's the correct tool.

Option 3: Multi-day campaigns

Most "I need to send 50,000 emails" use cases are actually "I need to send 50,000 emails over the next 30 days." A 50,000-message campaign at 1,500/day = 33 days, comfortably below limits and far better for deliverability than a 5-day blast at 10,000/day. Frequently asked questions

What is the Outlook send limit?

It depends on the account, and Microsoft publishes a number for only some of them. Microsoft 365 subscribers on Outlook.com: 5,000 recipients a day, 500 per message, and 1,000 a day to people you have not emailed before. Free Outlook.com accounts: no figure is published — Microsoft says only that limits are lower and vary with account history, and newer accounts are the most restricted. Microsoft 365 business and Exchange Online plans: 10,000 recipients a day, 500 per message by default (an admin can raise it to 1,000), 30 messages a minute.

What happens when I hit the Outlook send limit?

On a Microsoft 365 work or school mailbox, Microsoft returns a "submission quota exceeded" non-delivery report (code 5.2.2) and rejects new sends until the number of recipients you have messaged in the past 24 hours drops below the limit. It is a rolling window, not a reset at a fixed hour: Microsoft's own worked example has a sender who sends at 09:00 unable to send again until 09:00 the next day. On a personal Outlook.com account, Microsoft may instead pause sending and ask you to verify the account, with instructions sent to your Outlook inbox; follow them before you send again.

Can I increase my Outlook send limit?

On a Microsoft 365 work or school mailbox, the limit is enforced server-side and no user setting raises it. For Outlook.com, Microsoft says a new account's low quota is temporary and rises as the account establishes credibility, and it suggests a Microsoft 365 subscription, an up-to-date phone number and alternate email address on the account, and the Microsoft Authenticator app. New tenants also start with a lower tenant-wide limit on external recipients: 10% of the standard quota while the tenant is less than 31 days old, and 25% from day 31 to day 60. Organisations sending high-volume legitimate mail typically use a transactional service like Microsoft Azure Communication Services, SendGrid, or Mailgun for that traffic — not their primary Outlook mailbox.

Does sending slowly avoid the limit?

Slowing the per-minute rate avoids the throughput cap where one is published (30/min on business plans) but does not raise the daily total. If your daily ceiling is below the size of your list, you need a multi-day campaign or a different sending channel.

Will my account get suspended for sending too much?

On a work or school mailbox, hitting the daily limit by itself doesn't suspend the account — Microsoft refuses new sends until the 24-hour window clears. A personal Outlook.com account may instead be paused until you verify it. Suspension typically happens when a high spam-complaint rate, abuse reports, or a sudden burst pattern triggers Microsoft's anti-abuse systems. Slow, personalized, paced sending lowers that risk but does not remove it, especially from a new personal Outlook.com account.
